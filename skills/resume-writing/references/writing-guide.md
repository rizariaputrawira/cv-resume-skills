# Industry résumé writing guide

## Harvard guidance and its limits

[Harvard MCS's College résumé guide](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/) recommends concise, specific, active, fact-based language; demonstrated results; headings ordered by importance; reverse chronology within sections; consistent formatting; and balanced white space. It advises against personal pronouns, pictures, age/gender, and reference lists in its US-focused résumé context. It explicitly notes that country conventions differ.

Harvard's AI advice is to use AI for editing and brainstorming, not as the primary author. Start from the candidate's own content, preserve their meaning, and have them review the result. Do not describe an AI-produced draft as university-approved or a substitute for candidate authorship.

These are career recommendations, not a certification or universal specification. A fixed font, “skills must come first,” an exact years-of-experience cutoff, mandatory metrics, and one bullet formula are not established by this guide. The defaults below are this project's practical choices unless attributed otherwise.

## Structure by audience

Use conventional, clear headings. Choose relevant sections rather than filling every possible section.

| Context | Practical starting order |
| --- | --- |
| Student / recent graduate | Contact; Education; relevant Experience and/or Projects; Skills; relevant Leadership/Activities |
| Experienced applicant | Contact; optional targeted Summary; Experience; relevant Skills/Projects; Education |
| Career change | Contact; optional evidence-based Summary; relevant Experience/Projects; Skills; Education, ordered for the target role |

Contact information belongs in the document body, not a word processor's header, footer, or floating text box. Include supplied name, professional email, phone when appropriate, and relevant professional links. City/region is normally sufficient; do not request a full home address by default. Do not invent contact details or links. Ask for missing essential contact information before calling a document submission-ready.

Do not label someone a graduate before their degree is awarded. Preserve “expected” graduation, “in progress,” and expired versus active certification status. Include grades only if provided and useful; retain their original scale rather than inventing an equivalency.

Use reverse chronology within sections. Show actual role dates consistently, retain concurrent roles, and distinguish promotions when relevant. Clarify conflicting dates; do not conceal gaps by altering dates or inventing a filler role. An optional summary must add supported, role-specific information, not a generic personality claim or unheld job title.

## Write evidence-backed bullets

Use **action + work/context + contribution or outcome**, including method and scope when meaningful. STAR, CAR, and XYZ can help develop a bullet but are optional drafting aids, not mandatory sentence structures. Do not force a metric into every bullet.

- State what the candidate personally did; distinguish led, contributed, supported, and owned.
- Name technologies, methods, users, or domain only when supported and relevant. Do not infer a regulated compliance claim from a sector such as healthcare or finance.
- Describe a qualitative result when no reliable metric exists. A concrete deliverable or responsibility can be useful evidence without a claimed business outcome.
- Use present tense for ongoing responsibilities and past tense for completed work; keep punctuation and date style consistent. Neither a specific dash character nor a blanket past-tense rule is an established universal ATS requirement.
- Use direct verbs and readable language. Expand unfamiliar abbreviations on first use; retain recognizable, accurate technical names. Harvard's advice against abbreviations is a clarity principle here, not a ban on standard terms such as SQL.

If the candidate asks to quantify an unmeasured result, first look for supportable scope (frequency, volume, time period, audience, or deliverables). Request the missing baseline, denominator, or measurement only if it would support a useful claim. A range or lower bound still needs evidence: do not round a hunch down and call it a minimum, or turn a weekly count into unique annual users without evidence about repeat users and the observed period. Use a qualitative contribution if no defensible measure exists.

### Synthetic example: qualitative evidence

Candidate note: “I built SQL checks for duplicate records in the monthly sales report. Finance used the checks. We did not measure time saved.”

Supported rewrite: “Built SQL checks to flag duplicate records in monthly sales reports for the finance team.”

Unsupported rewrite: “Automated financial reporting, saving 40 hours monthly and eliminating all data errors.” The note supports neither savings, complete automation, nor elimination of all errors.

### Synthetic example: calculated metric

Candidate-confirmed measurements: median report runtime fell from 20 to 12 minutes for the same workload after the candidate's indexing change.

Supported rewrite: “Reduced median report runtime from 20 to 12 minutes by adding database indexes.” A derived 40% reduction is also valid if the baseline, measurement comparability, and attribution are confirmed: `(20 - 12) / 20 × 100 = 40%`. Do not turn median into average, runtime reduction into cost savings, or a team result into individual ownership.

## Tailoring without keyword manipulation

Read the actual job description, separate required and preferred qualifications, and map them to candidate evidence. Prioritize the most relevant supported examples, including transferable evidence from non-work settings when clearly labeled. Use an exact term or expanded acronym when it is accurate and useful; do not insert every synonym or repeat keywords to chase a density target.

Do not change historical job titles, claim an unheld certification, infer proficiency from a job requirement, hide white text, add invisible keywords, or paste the job description into the résumé. The separate gap analysis may mention missing requirements; the résumé must not claim them.

For a career transition, map **target capability → actual example → transferable contribution**. Translate jargon, not the role itself. For example, a retail associate who prepared stock reports can show reporting experience without becoming an “Inventory Analyst.” Label coursework, volunteer projects, or open-source work honestly. When citing GitHub work, state the specific contribution (such as tests, documentation, fixes, or release tooling) and link to public evidence only if appropriate. Do not turn use of GitHub into experience building CI/CD or maintaining a repository.

## Length, locale, and language

A one-page résumé is a useful starting point for many students; two pages can be appropriate for substantial relevant experience. These are project defaults, not Harvard certification rules. Follow explicit employer/program requirements, retain meaningful evidence, and edit content before reducing readability. Do not decide length solely from years worked.

Ask for the target country when locale materially changes the result. [Europass guidance](https://europass.europa.eu/en/create-europass-cv) recommends tailoring, clear language, and reverse chronology but also suggests a photo, unlike Harvard's US-focused guidance. This disagreement is a reason to resolve locale and application requirements, not to claim that every European application requires a photo. Default to omitting photos and sensitive demographic information unless the candidate's explicit request and destination context justify them; never add them from inference.

For translation, preserve names, dates, original qualifications, achievement scope, and proficiency levels. Explain an unfamiliar qualification accurately rather than awarding a new title or degree equivalence. Use the destination language naturally and check diacritics in exports.
